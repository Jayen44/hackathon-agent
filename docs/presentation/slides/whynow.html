Where we start

Start in the United States: fleets at scale and far richer data

Waymo: driverless

About 500,000 paid rides a week in 10 cities

About 200 million driverless miles a year; 271 million in total by June 2026

The real driverless claims, and the cases where the careful-driver test is clearest

Tesla: driver-assist

8 billion miles on its supervised driving system by February 2026, on Tesla’s own figures

The volume wedge. Liability sits mostly with the driver, so we use it for triage and screening, not guaranteed recovery

What that gives us

Public crash reports under a federal reporting order, which the regulator says may be incomplete

Real crashes to check the simulator against: it reproduces 88% of crashes and 94% of near-crashes in a public study

United Kingdom next: 15 supervised vehicles and none on the government’s self-driving list today, so little volume. We are built for it when the Automated Vehicles Act 2024 regime is live (expected late 2027).

Sources: market/MARKET.md (operator figures, Tesla’s own); eval/REAL_CRASH_EVAL.md (100-Car study, public domain). Automated Vehicle Recovery Desk

Why the United States first. Waymo runs about 500,000 paid rides a week in 10 cities, roughly 200 million driverless miles a year, which is the volume of real driverless claims. Tesla's supervised driving system has 8 billion miles on Tesla's own figures: that is the volume wedge, with liability mostly on the driver, so we use it for triage and screening. The United States also publishes crash reports under a federal reporting order, which the regulator itself says may be incomplete, and public real-crash studies to validate against: our simulator reproduces 88 percent of crashes and 94 percent of near-crashes in the 100-Car study. The United Kingdom has 15 supervised vehicles and none on the government's list, so little volume until its regime is live, expected late 2027 from secondary reporting. Scoring cue: business viability, pitch.
